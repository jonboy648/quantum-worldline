Where this began

It started with a question that sounds like science fiction.

Could the fabric of space — and gravity itself — not be fundamental at all, but something that emerges from the way quantum information is woven together?

This isn't a fringe idea. It's one of the most active frontiers in theoretical physics, known as holography: the conjecture that a universe with gravity can be perfectly encoded on its lower-dimensional boundary, like a 3D hologram stored on a 2D film. The deep version of the claim is that geometry is made of entanglement — that distance, curvature, and gravitational attraction are bookkeeping for how quantum systems are correlated.

The trouble with a beautiful idea is testing it. This project set out to do exactly that — not with a particle accelerator, but with a quantum tensor network simulated on ordinary computers, asking a sharp, falsifiable question: if you build the simplest possible "toy universe" this way, does a real gravitational force appear inside it — and does it have the precise mathematical form Einstein's theory demands?

The core idea, in plain terms

A chain of quantum bits, folded into a space.

Picture a row of quantum bits — a "spin chain" — tuned to a delicate critical point, the knife-edge between two phases of matter. A mathematical structure called MERA (a multi-scale entanglement renormalization ansatz) organizes that chain into layers, each one coarser than the last.

Here is the remarkable part: those layers behave like the extra dimension of a curved space. The flat quantum chain lives on the boundary; the stack of layers builds an emergent, hyperbolic "bulk" — the same geometry that describes the space around a black hole. The boundary is the hologram; the bulk is the gravity it encodes.

If holography is right, then putting two disturbances on the boundary chain should make them attract each other through the bulk — a genuine emergent force. The whole project hangs on one measurable thing: the exact way that attraction weakens with distance.

The headline result

The force fades as distance to the fourth power. That number is the whole game.

In Einstein's gravity, the carrier of the force between these particular excitations is the stress-energy tensor — and the math of the boundary theory makes an unambiguous prediction: the attraction must weaken as 1 / distance⁴. Not squared (that would mean an ordinary scalar field). Not exponential. Exactly the fourth power.

Two more signatures

It wasn't just the force. Two other fingerprints of curved space showed up.

A signal travels like light through curved space.

Send a ripple along the boundary chain and time how long it takes to cross. Holography predicts that time is set by the bulk geometry — specifically T = (π/2) × ℓ, where ℓ is the emergent radius of curvature. We measured it and got agreement to about 1.2%. The signal moves exactly as if it were crossing a real curved spacetime.

A massless boundary excitation gains weight in the bulk.

An excitation that is "massless" on the boundary should acquire a specific effective mass once viewed as a particle living in the curved bulk. The number predicted by the conformal field theory is 0.693. The number the tensor network actually produced was 0.670 — a match to 3.5%.

Three different observables — a force, a travel time, and a mass — all independently pointing at the same emergent geometry. That convergence is what turned a hopeful idea into a publishable result.

The part most people skip

We spent as much effort trying to break the result as to find it.

A finding you haven't attacked isn't a finding — it's a hope. So the project ran a standing adversarial process: every promising result was handed to independent reviewers whose only job was to kill it.

A tempting shortcut, ruled out
An "integral-geometry" route looked like it reproduced the force — until the full curve was checked and disagreed catastrophically. Killed. A coincidence in one number had masqueraded as physics.
A hardware experiment, called off
A planned $115 run on a real quantum computer was cancelled when a smoke test showed the measurement was structurally wrong before any money was spent. The gate worked exactly as designed.
Our own published number, corrected
A later, finer simulation showed one headline precision claim (a "0.72%" agreement) was a measurement-grid artifact; the honest value is ~1.2%. The result survives — it just got more honest. Logged in an errata file.
A self-inflicted error, caught
A full-project audit found that our own verification layer had once inverted a baseline comparison. We caught it ourselves, before any reviewer could. That's the system working.

This is the texture of real research: most ideas die, the survivors are stronger for it, and the discipline is in writing down the deaths.

The newest chapter

Can we do this on a real quantum computer?

The simulations are settled. The frontier question is whether the force can be detected on actual quantum hardware — the kind of "experimental quantum gravity" that, to our knowledge, no one has done yet.

The honest blocker has been noise: today's machines fumble enough gates that the signal can drown. So the recent work attacked the cost. An adaptive circuit-design method (ADAPT-VQE) found a recipe that prepares the right quantum state and carries the force in just 33 delicate operations — versus 451 for the original network. A genuine ~14× reduction, verified by a real compiler against a real hardware gate set.

It isn't a clean win yet — the cheap recipe is fragile to imperfect calibration, and it stops scaling past the smallest size. But the door that looked welded shut turned out to be merely heavy. And a new route opened: a 260-qubit neutral-atom machine with a collaboration program, which could run this physics at proper scale without the usual institutional gatekeeping.

What it adds up to

A small, honest "yes" to a very large question.

In the simplest universe we can build out of quantum entanglement, a gravitational force appears — with the exact distance-law Einstein's theory requires, confirmed by three independent measurements and backed by two more geometric signatures.

That doesn't prove our universe works this way. It proves the mechanism is real and measurable in a controlled setting — and it lays down a concrete, falsifiable path toward seeing it on quantum hardware. The work is published with an open dataset, every dead end is recorded alongside every success, and the next experiment is already scoped.

"Geometry is made of entanglement" went from a slogan to a number you can measure: 4.0.
