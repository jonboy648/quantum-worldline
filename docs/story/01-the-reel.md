It started with a reel on Instagram.

A woman sitting at a table with a notebook open in front of her, talking straight into her phone. The caption said it was for the physics nerds and
the spiritual people who like the idea that time is an illusion.

She had a psychology degree and was taking physics classes because she wanted to. And she had two
ideas she could barely sit still for.

The first was about time. Physicists, she said, mostly agree that time does not flow the way we feel
it flowing. Past, present and future are not a moving spotlight. They are all there, all at once,
and the spotlight is us. If that is true, then a person is not a thing that moves through time. A
person is a shape *in* time. Every version of you, from the first breath to the last one, is one
connected object, and what you call "now" is a slice of it. She described it as a worm. Not a
flattering word, but nobody has improved on it. When you remember something, you are touching
another part of the worm. When you get a flash of where your life is supposed to go, maybe you are
touching a part you have not lived yet.

The second idea came from her calculus class. Her professor had been teaching triple integrals, and
had a habit of calling one piece of them "the shadow."

Hold a ball in the sun and its shadow on the ground is a flat disc. The shadow is the ball with one
dimension squeezed out of it. Calculus uses the same picture. The simplest integral measures the
area under a curve, and the floor that area stands on is a piece of a line. A flat shape, with a
shadow one dimension lower. A double integral measures the volume under a surface, and the floor it
stands on is a flat region. A solid, with a flat shadow. A triple integral goes one step further than
anyone can draw. The floor it stands on is a solid region of ordinary space, and that makes the
solid region the shadow of something four-dimensional.

She put the two ideas together on camera, in real time, and you could watch it land on her: if a
three-dimensional solid can be the shadow of a four-dimensional thing, then maybe a
three-dimensional person is one too. The worm casts a shadow. The shadow is you, today, at this table.

"That literally gives me chills," she said, and then she said that this is what everything you are
experiencing right now would be: the projection of that four-dimensional thing into this
three-dimensional world.

A shadow and a slice are not the same thing. A shadow presses the whole object flat, every moment of
a life at once. You at this table are a slice, one moment cut out of the worm. The reel slides from
one to the other. As a picture it works anyway.

She was not claiming to have discovered anything. She was a student having the kind of moment
students are supposed to have, out loud, on the internet. The block universe is a hundred years old.
The picture of a person as a worldline through spacetime is in every relativity textbook, and
"worldline" is where the name of this whole project comes from. The shadow of a higher-dimensional
object is a metaphor mathematicians reach for constantly. None of it was new.

What was new was that Jon could not put it down.

Jon has ADHD, and he had a theory about it that he had talked about often. The usual story is that
ADHD is a lack of attention. His version was that the attention is not missing, it is spread out. A
focused mind picks one thought and holds it. His holds several at once, none of them chosen yet,
until something makes it pick. And a mind holding several things at once is a mind that notices when
two of them match. He thought that was why people with ADHD are so often good at spotting patterns.
The same looseness that makes it hard to stay on one thing is what lets two far-apart ideas touch.

He also knew what that sounded like. In the strange parts of physics, a thing can be in more than
one state at once until you look at it, and the word for that is superposition. He had wondered for
years whether the resemblance was more than a figure of speech. He had talked about it often, but
never with anyone who could tell him whether it was nonsense.

That is why the reel landed. It was a pattern match. A worm from a physics class and a shadow from a
calculus class, two ideas from two different rooms, touching. It was exactly the kind of thing his
mind does.

The shadow landed for a second reason too. It was math. It came out of a classroom, not out of a
feeling, and it hinted that the whole picture might be something a person could check.

And that led to a third thing, which was not in the video at all. It was the question underneath it.
If any of this is real, and not just a good way of talking, then somewhere there should be math that
says so. Not a metaphor. A calculation, with a number at the end, that comes out one way if the world
is like that and another way if it is not.

That question is the whole reason this project exists. He asked it to an AI later, in a long
conversation he still has saved, and it is the question he has been asking for the year and a half
since, with a lot more machinery than he had that night. The machinery is what most of this story is
about: what got built, what it said, and the long, specific, humbling process of finding out which
parts of what it said were true.

The honest answer, as of this writing, is that the math has said no more often than it has said yes.
Here is what that means.

What got built is a toy universe inside a computer: a row of tiny magnets, each one pointing up or
down, tuned to the exact point where they cannot make up their minds. Physicists care about that
point because of a famous idea that a system like it behaves as if an extra dimension of space were
hidden inside it, the way a hologram hides depth inside a flat sheet. If the shadow has real math
behind it anywhere, that is the best-known place to look.

Every test began by writing down, before the computer ran, what the answer should be and what answer
would count as failure. A yes means it passed when it could have failed. A no means it failed.

The yeses are like building a model airplane and checking it against the blueprint. The toy was asked
questions the textbooks already know the answers to, and it gave the textbook answers, very
precisely. That proves the ruler is good. It does not prove anything about worms.

The noes are the ones that touch the reel, and each one has its own chapter. They have been more
interesting than the yeses. If you want to know whether you are the
shadow of a worm, this story cannot tell you. It can tell you what it took to ask the question
properly, and what happened when he did.

The reel is still up. Its author goes by lo, with a butterfly, and calls herself a professional leap
encourager. Jon has never spoken to her.
