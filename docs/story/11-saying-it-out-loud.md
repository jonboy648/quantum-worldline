# 11. Saying it out loud

*Draft 1, 2026-09-07. Jon's first person. Sources: the Zenodo v5 correction notice
(`research/tools/zenodo-v5/notice.md`), the adversarial review of 2026-09-06 and 2026-09-07,
`docs/governance/PLAN-2026-09-06.md`, memory `zenodo_doi.md`.*

---

By the first week of September the situation was this. The paper had been rejected, so no journal
was waiting for a correction. The place where our headline number still stood, as a fact, with a
permanent identifier attached, was the archive record we had made ourselves. (A public copy of the
code repository said the same thing in its front page; we found that a day later and took it
private the same day the correction went up.) Anyone who searched for the work would find version four, would read
in its abstract that the exponent was three point nine two and matched the geometry to two
percent, and would have no way of knowing that the author no longer believed a word of it.

There was no one to make us fix that. That is the thing about the locked front door: it locks the
back door too. Nobody was going to write and demand a retraction, because nobody had read it. We
could have let it sit. I want to be honest that the thought occurred, and that the machine, to its
credit, named the thought before I did and said why it was wrong.

So we wrote a correction and published it against our own record.

The mechanics are simple. The archive keeps every version forever, and a new version can be
anything, including a notice that the old versions were wrong. We wrote the notice as the first two
pages of the document and as the record's description, so that it is the first thing anyone sees
before the manuscript, which is left unaltered underneath. It withdraws the exponent. It withdraws
the claim that the exponent was universal, since the other two chains never measured it. It
withdraws the third evaluation, whose data change sign inside the window it was fit over. It
withdraws the registered test, because the test compared a thing to itself. It withdraws the
statement that the light candidate was ruled out, which had been announced at twelve standard
deviations using an error bar four times too small. It states, in a table, what was claimed, what
it was, what it is now, and why.

It also says, in plain words, that no corrected number is established. We had one, two point six,
from the plateau on the open chain, and the temptation to put it in the headline of the correction
was real. A retraction that offers a new number in the same confident voice as the old one is just
the same mistake in a different font. So the notice says the retraction is certain and the
replacement is not, and it says what the plus or minus means, which is the spread of a flat region
and not a statistical error, and it says the bootstrap error on the original fit was four times
larger than what we printed.

Before it went up, we did the thing we should have done before version one. We gave the draft to a
reviewer who had no stake in it, a separate instance of the machine with no memory of the project,
told to find anything false, overstated, understated, or missing, with every record it needed to
check against. It came back with fourteen findings. Two were blocking: the abstract's second
evaluation had not been withdrawn, and neither had the exclusion of the light candidate. Both were
right. Both went in. The reviewer read the revised notice and said it could go.

Then I read it, and said publish, and it was published on the seventh of September. The record now
carries the notice, the re-analysis, the pre-registrations with their timestamps, every driver, and
the test suite from the last chapter, so that anyone who wants to check the retraction can rerun
it.

Publishing it was not hard, and the reason it was not is the reason I am writing this at all. By the time the notice was written, the wrong number had stopped
being mine. It belonged to a version of the process that had not yet learned to ask whether a
result could have come out any other way. Retracting it felt less like confession than like
finally answering a question I had asked a machine a year and a half earlier. Can math prove any
of this? Not that. Here is how we know.
