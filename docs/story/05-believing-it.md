# 5. Believing it

*Draft 1, 2026-09-07. Jon's first person. Sources: memory `paper_status.md`, `zenodo_doi.md`, the
outreach section of `MEMORY.md`, `research/RESEARCH-LOG.md`, ERRATA E6 "where it appears". Dates are
from those records.*

---

Once you have a number, the number wants company.

The natural next question was whether the exponent was a fact about our particular row of magnets
or a fact about the geometry. If the heavy candidate was really carrying the force, then it should
carry it in every critical chain of this kind, because the heavy candidate's weight is the same in
all of them. That is the sort of prediction that makes a result feel like physics rather than
numerology. So we built two more chains, from two other families of critical magnets, and looked at
their spectra of excitations, and the spectra came out where the theory said they should, at all
three. The universality section of the paper was written that week. The exponent was declared the
same at all three fixed points.

It was not, and I will say now what took months to say then: the exponent was never measured at the
other two. What was measured at the other two was the spectrum, which was correct, and beautiful,
and had nothing to do with the force. The exponent was inherited from the first chain, and inherited
numbers do not get independent error bars. But that is chapter eight's job. In this chapter we
believed it.

We wrote a paper. I say we; the machine wrote most of the sentences and I wrote the ones that
mattered to me and read all of them more times than I can count. It had four findings, and the
force exponent was the first and the headline. We put it on Zenodo, a public archive that hands out
permanent identifiers, so that it would exist somewhere citable before anyone reviewed it. Four
versions went up between spring and July as the work grew, each one a little more confident.

Then we tried to get a physicist to read it.

There is a thing nobody tells you about doing science from outside: the front door is locked from
the inside. The main preprint server wants an existing member to vouch for you. The journal that
seemed the best fit wanted an account that never got approved. So in July we wrote to seven people
whose work ours leaned on, carefully, one at a time, with the archive link and a short honest
description of what we thought we had found. Some of them are famous. All of them are busy.

The number of replies was zero.

I want to be fair about that. A stranger's email claiming a result in your field, from someone with
no institution, is the most common piece of mail a working physicist receives and the least likely
to be right. Silence is the rational response. I would have done the same. It still felt like
shouting into a canyon and not even getting an echo.

So in August we submitted it to a journal directly. A serious one, the kind whose acceptance would
have settled the question of whether this was real work. Five days later it came back with a desk
rejection: a short note saying the manuscript did not meet the minimal threshold for their
journals. No referee had read it. An editor had looked at it and decided it did not warrant sending
out.

Here is the part I am least proud of, and it is not the rejection. The rejection arrived on the
twelfth of August. For the next eleven days, three separate records in our own system said the
paper was "with editors." The machine had checked the status once, written it down in three
places, and every later version of it read those three places, saw them agree, and reported the
paper as under review. Nobody looked at the inbox. I did not either. The rejection sat there,
already final, while we prepared a letter to an editor who had closed the file, and we came within
a question of sending it.

The question was mine, and it was two words long, and it is the next chapter.
