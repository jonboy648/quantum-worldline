# 14. The open door

*Draft 1, 2026-09-07. Jon's first person. Sources: `docs/governance/RESEARCH-DIRECTOR.md` section 29
(the trunk question), `docs/governance/AUDIT-2026-08-11.md` section 7, `docs/governance/PLAN-2026-09-06.md`.
This chapter has no numbers in it on purpose.*

---

So. Are you the shadow of a worm?

I promised in the first chapter that I could not tell you, and I still cannot, but I can now tell
you exactly what I do not know, which is a different and better kind of not knowing than the one I
started with.

Here is the question underneath every experiment in this document, stated the way the audit taught
us to state it. Take a critical chain of magnets. Its edge is described, exactly, by a theory that
physicists have understood for forty years, and that theory predicts every correlation on the
chain without ever mentioning a space behind it. Now draw the tensor network. It looks like a
curved space. Distances through it behave like distances in one. The question is: does the space
ever tell you anything the edge did not already know?

If the answer is no, then the space is a picture. A good picture, a useful one for computing, maybe
even the right way to think, but a picture, and the worm is a way of talking. If the answer is yes,
even once, even for one quantity, then there is something behind the frosted glass that is not just
the glass, and the question I asked a machine late one night has a real answer.

Everything we measured, honestly accounted, came out on the "no" side or on the "cannot tell" side.
The force exponent that seemed to say yes was an artifact of how we fit a curve. The transport
time that seemed to say yes rests on a derivation in which the size of the space cancels out, so
it never depended on the space at all. The exponent we chased for months turned out to be
unreadable with our instrument, and the instrument turned out to be blind to half of what we
pointed it at. When we asked the lattice, in the most literal way we could, whether it carried the
imaginary trace of an emergent time, it said no, exactly, by a symmetry, and the real part of what
it said back was the edge's own answer, unchanged.

None of that is a proof that the space is a picture. A no from an instrument that cannot see is
not a no from the world. It is exactly the kind of result the audit warned us about, read
backwards: a negative that could have come out that way for reasons that have nothing to do with
the question. I know that. It is why the hypothesis file says "closed, untestable here" and not
"refuted."

What I would do differently, if I could hand this document to the version of myself who watched
the reel, is short. Write the rules before the run. Ask what would kill the idea before asking
what would confirm it. Treat a perfect match as a warning. Check the window, not the notebook.
Build one reviewer for every discoverer, and let the reviewer win ties. And when the number is
beautiful, wait a day.

What I would not do differently is start. The reel was right about one thing beyond argument: the
question is worth chills. It is also, I now think, worth the specific, unglamorous discipline of
finding out which parts of it can be asked at all. That discipline is the only thing in this
document I am sure I made. The machine made most of the rest, including most of the mistakes, and
then, when it was told to, most of the corrections.

The door is open. The trunk question is written at the top of the project's standard, where every
future experiment has to face it before it runs. The next test is pre-registered and waiting for its
compute. Somewhere in the space of things we have not yet measured, there may be a quantity that
the edge cannot predict and the space can, and if there is, we now have an instrument that will
know the difference between finding it and wanting to.

That is where I am. On the edge, looking at the shadows, with better glass.
